    How Omusaayi works

    Omusaayi means blood in Luganda. The inner lining of the lower eyelid (palpebral conjunctiva) is rich in blood vessels and carries no skin pigment, so its redness tracks haemoglobin in women of every skin tone. Health workers already check it by eye for pallor. Omusaayi measures that colour with a phone camera instead.

    1. Colour correction. A white paper in the photo sets the white balance, which cancels most of the difference between phones and lighting.
    2. Feature extraction. Inside the marked lining, the app drops glare and shadow pixels, then computes the erythema index (100 × log₁₀ R/G), the share of strongly red pixels, and CIELAB redness (a*).
    3. Calibrated estimate. A ridge regression, refit on this device from HemoCue-paired readings, turns those three numbers into an Hb estimate with an error range taken from leave-one-out testing.
    4. Action. The estimate is graded against WHO cut-offs for the person's group, and the app suggests a next step and writes a referral note.

    Safety by design

    • All image processing happens in the browser. Photos are never uploaded or saved.
    • Saved readings hold colour numbers, a client code and a lab Hb. No names and no images.
    • Nothing is saved without a recorded consent tick.
    • Every result is labelled as a screen. Borderline and positive results always point to a blood test.

    Why Uganda needs it

    • Pooled data from 17 Ugandan studies (14,410 pregnant women) put anaemia in pregnancy at about 30%.
    • Facility assessments found gaps in haemoglobin testing, especially point-of-care testing, in Ugandan antenatal care.
    • In Kamuli district, haemoglobin was among the least-done antenatal tests, held back by stock-outs of kits and reagents.
    • Adolescent girls are largely missing from national anaemia surveys, even though school studies find high rates.

    Limits

    Phone-based Hb estimates are less precise than a blood test and are best at catching moderate and severe anaemia. The example model in this prototype is trained on simulated numbers and must not be used on real clients. A Ugandan validation study against HemoCue is the next step.
