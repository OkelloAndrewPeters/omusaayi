    How Omusaayi works
    
      
    Omusaayi means blood in Luganda. The inner lining of the lower eyelid (palpebral conjunctiva) is rich in blood vessels and carries no skin pigment, so its redness tracks haemoglobin in women of every skin tone. Health workers already check it by eye for pallor. Omusaayi measures that colour with a phone camera, then makes sure a positive screen leads to a blood test, treatment and a repeat check.
    
      
    Screening
    
      
      
        
    1. Colour correction. A white paper in the photo sets the white balance, which cancels most of the difference between phones and lighting.
      2. 
        
    2. Feature extraction. Inside the marked lining, the app drops glare and shadow pixels, then computes the erythema index (100 × log₁₀ R/G), the share of strongly red pixels, and CIELAB redness (a*).
      3. 
        
    3. Calibrated estimate. A ridge regression, refit on the device from HemoCue-paired readings, turns those numbers into an Hb estimate with an error range from leave-one-out testing.
      4. 
        
    4. Grading. The estimate is graded against WHO cut-offs for the client's group. Results within the error range of the cut-off count as borderline and are referred too.
      5. 
      
    
      
    Referral and follow-up
    
      
      
        
    1. Referral. A positive or borderline screen creates a referral with a due date set by severity: severe today, moderate within 3 days, borderline within 7 days, mild by the next antenatal visit (14 days).
      2. 
        
    2. Confirmation. The health worker records the confirmed blood Hb and whether treatment started.
      3. 
        
    3. Repeat check. Treated clients come back on the worklist after 4 weeks for a repeat Hb. Clients who are still anaemic stay on the list.
      4. 
        
    4. Accountability. Overdue referrals are flagged. Clients not reached are recorded as lost to follow-up, so every positive screen ends in a known outcome.
      5. 
      
    
      
    Programme intelligence
    
      
    The Insights tab turns the same records into the care cascade, referral completion and loss to follow-up by facility, screen-positive rates by group, monthly trends, and a DHIS2-ready monthly aggregate. No individual data leaves the phone unless a team member exports it.

    Safety by design
    
      
      
        
    • All image processing happens in the browser. Photos are never uploaded or saved.
      • 
        
    • Records hold colour numbers, a client code, results and follow-up dates. No names and no images.
      • 
        
    • Nothing is saved without a recorded consent tick, including guardian consent for girls under 18.
      • 
        
    • Every result is labelled as a screen. Borderline and positive results always point to a blood test.
      • 
        
    • Reports share only aggregate counts.
      • 
      
    
      
    Why Uganda needs it
    
      
      
        
    • Pooled data from 17 Ugandan studies (14,410 pregnant women) put anaemia in pregnancy at about 30%.
      • 
        
    • Facility assessments found gaps in haemoglobin testing, especially point-of-care testing, in Ugandan antenatal care.
      • 
        
    • In Kamuli district, haemoglobin was among the least-done antenatal tests, held back by stock-outs of kits and reagents.
      • 
        
    • Adolescent girls are largely missing from national anaemia surveys, even though school studies find high rates.
      • 
      
    
      
    Limits
    
      
    Phone-based Hb estimates are less precise than a blood test and are best at catching moderate and severe anaemia. The example model and the example follow-up records in this prototype are simulated and must not be used for real clients or reporting. A Ugandan validation study against HemoCue is the next step.
